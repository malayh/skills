# Tactics — value-writing companion

Loaded on demand by the value-writing skill. Sentence mechanics, word-level
moves, named structural patterns, and coherence techniques. Reach for this
file during the rewrite step when surface-level fixes need precision.

## Word-level: value-bearing vocabulary

Words do value-work. Most drafts are full of words that occupy space and
carry no value-charge. Recognize two kinds to use, two kinds to kill.

### Community-signaling words
Prove the community cares about the topic.
"widely," "accepted," "established," "reported," "documented," "settled,"
"orthodox," "consensus."

### Tension-signaling words
Destabilize the apparent settled state.
"however," "but," "although," "yet," "nonetheless," "inconsistent,"
"anomalous," "puzzling," "paradoxically," "in tension with," "unsettled,"
"unresolved."

### Charged nouns and verbs
Pick vocabulary with cognitive weight.
"crisis" over "situation." "seize," "annex," "claim" over "take."
"tool" over "idea" or "concept" (tools imply use).

### Words to cut (low-value, junior register)
- "important" / "important study" — empty signal.
- "new" / "original" — positivistic; replace with "valuable" or "consequential."
- "interesting" — interesting *to the writer*.
- "contribute to the conversation" / "add my voice" / "join the discourse"
  — invites the reader to say "we don't care."
- "perhaps," "consider," "might be worth," "could potentially" — hedges
  that drain tension.

### Words to cut (continuity vocabulary, kills tension)
"extends," "builds on," "consistent with," "in line with," "as previously
established," "following on from." These come from a dead model of
knowledge-as-accumulation. Hunt-and-replace at revision time with tension
words.

### Flow vs tension transitions
"And," "because," "if," "unless" create flow but no tension.
"But," "however," "although" create flow *and* tension. The "use
transitions" rule most writers absorbed picked the flow half. Use the
tension half.

### Code-word workflow
Spend ~15 min/week reading 1–2 articles in the target community. Circle
every word doing value-work. Build a personal list. Paste from it at
revision time. If you can't underline 10 value-words in your first 2
paragraphs, pull them in from the list.

The **code-word-extractor** sibling skill automates the corpus side: feed
it URLs, PDFs, or pasted text and get a categorized lexicon back. The
hand-curation step still matters — the extractor produces a draft list,
not a final one.

## Sentence mechanics

### Focus position = subject
The subject of a sentence tells the reader what the sentence is "about" —
they process the rest in terms of it. Wrong subject means the reader
re-aims mid-sentence. Friction.

**The single exercise McEnerney charges money to walk through**: underline
every sentence subject in the draft. For each, ask: does my reader care
about this thing? If not, rewrite to move what they care about into
subject position.

### Stress position = end
The end of an English sentence carries extra stress. Value-bearing words
belong at sentence ends, not buried mid-sentence. Many short sentences =
many stress points = nothing important. One long, well-managed sentence
with sub-stresses building to a final stress can carry more value than
five short ones.

### Sentence length is not a rule
Long sentences need higher skill (value distributed throughout). Short
sentences fit distracted, mobile, or skeptical readers. Same applies to
*every* taught "rule": no passive voice, no starting with "and," no split
infinitives, max 6 sentences per paragraph. None are rules. They're
heuristics for specific readers. Trace each to a reading process or ignore.

### Subject patterns: same / chaining / random
Three patterns across consecutive sentences:
- **Same subject** repeated — most coherent. Lincoln's "we" across the
  Gettysburg Address.
- **Chaining** (end of one sentence → subject of next) — works for ~4
  sentences before drift.
- **Random** — disastrous. The high-school "vary your sentence openings"
  rule causes incoherence.

Diagnostic: circle every sentence subject in order. If they vary randomly,
fix.

### The 10% rule
Lincoln says "we dedicate here" in some form 28 times in 278 words.
~10% of the words are doing thematic work. Most writers' most important
words appear exactly once.

Identify the 3 words the text most depends on. Count them. If under ~5%
of total word count, repeat — or substitute audience-associated cousins
("dedicate" / "consecrate" / "hallow" / "devotion" in Lincoln). Feels
repetitive to the writer, reads as coherent to the reader.

### Concision is reader-time, not word-count
"The dog chased the cat" is *less* concise for a reader who cares about
cats than "the cat was chased by the dog." Passive voice can be more
concise. Concision = cognitive processing time per unit of value, not a
word count.

## Named structural patterns

### Problem → solution (the basic engagement loop)
The moment a reader perceives a problem, their head is unconsciously
expecting a solution. That expectation is engagement. Used in the western
tradition for 3,000 years. "Sing Muse, the rage of Achilles." Children's
books open with a problem.

Replace "thesis" with "solution to a problem" and rewrite the opening to
construct the problem first.

### Working backward: solution → problem
Given a thesis you want to convey:
1. State the solution.
2. Ask: what problem would this solve?
3. State the problem. Construct the opening around it.

When stuck, take a key word from the thesis and try its opposite.
"Optimal" → "is there a suboptimal version?" If yes, that's your problem.
Works without subject-matter expertise.

### Manifest + Critical (two-problem structure)
- **Manifest problem**: the obvious one everyone sees (pager fatigue,
  attrition, AI hype, climate change).
- **Critical problem**: the deeper one that PREVENTS solving the manifest.

Pattern: "Yes, there's the obvious X — but we have a second problem that
will block solving X unless we address it first." Carries an "I see deeper
than the rest of you" connotation. Common in policy memos, RFCs,
analytical blog posts, investor decks. Marx is the master.

### Multiple problems, one solution
High-skill move: construct several distinct problems all resolving to the
same action. Lincoln: nation-survival + democracy-survival + personal-grief,
all resolving to "dedicate ourselves to the war." Built backward from the
solution. Easy to overdo and produce incoherence.

### Negation-then-affirmation ("not Y, it's X")
Pattern: "This is not about Y. This is not Z either. It's X." Malcolm X
uses it as an opener. Obama's "more perfect union" speech uses it ~30 times.

Each "not" creates a micro-problem ("then what?"); the affirmation
resolves it. Writers' instinct is to cut the negation half for concision —
wrong. The negation half *is* the value.

### "I thought X, was wrong, here's Y"
Sub-pattern. Powerful for celebration / positive content that would
otherwise land flat. Oprah on Rosa Parks: not "she was the personification
of grace" but "I thought she'd be 100 feet tall — but here was this petite
delicate lady who was the personification of grace."

Use for founder profiles, hiring announcements, "lessons learned" posts,
year-in-review content.

### Layered tensions (the lit-review / "as you know" paragraph)
- **Junior**: "X said A. Y said B. Z said C." (chronological summary)
- **Senior**: "X said A — but Y showed B, which puts A in tension — and
  then Z showed C, which deepens the tension because if Z is right, both
  X and Y misled us."

Lit-review as instability-amplifier, not summary. Applies to "as you know"
paragraphs, market-context paragraphs, "current state of the art" sections
in proposals.

### Parallelism with tension
Standard parallelism = same grammatical structure repeated. Powerful
parallelism = same structure with words in TENSION across slots:
- "with malice toward none, with charity for all" (malice/charity,
  none/all)
- "political oppression, economic exploitation, social degradation"
  (different verbs of harm, intensified by parallel form)

Flat parallelism does little. Parallelism with opposing or tense words
amplifies. Works in headlines, slogans, opening sentences, taglines.

## Coherence techniques

Coherence is *experiential*, not logical. Readers don't process a
thesis-and-supports hierarchy. They flow through the text in time.
"I-want-what's-next" repeated is what coherence feels like.

### Brought-in vs created-by-text coherence
- **Brought-in**: reader already knows the pattern. Past-present-future,
  birth-death-rebirth, three-act story. Fast and powerful when the
  audience shares the pattern; risky for mixed audiences.
- **Created-by-text**: the text trains the reader on a pattern. Sequence
  "1,1,2; 1,1,3; 1,1,4" → reader predicts "1,1,5" without prior knowledge.

For mixed audiences (the business-writing default), created-by-text is
more reliable.

### Five dimensions of coherence
Pick two or three and run them through a long piece:
- **Time**: past → present → future.
- **Space**: continent → nation → here (Lincoln says "here" 7+ times).
- **Myth/Cycle**: birth → death → rebirth.
- **Subject**: same subject repeated across sentences.
- **Theme**: same word or close associates repeated (the 10% rule applied).

### Thematic strings
Strings work via words the *audience* associates with the theme — not
synonyms in a dictionary, not the writer's conceptual relations. Obama's
"Union" theme accepts "together" and "truly one" but probably not
"society." A writerly judgment call, not a lookup.

## Point-first vs point-last

Default: lead with your point. ~19 out of 20 cases.

Exception: when revealing the point too early causes the reader to reject
it before reading the argument. James Fallows' 1984 op-ed reinstating the
draft — the headline would have killed it, so he buries the point at the
end after hooking liberal readers on adjacent problems.

Diagnostic: would putting the point in sentence 3 make this fail? If yes,
consider burying. If no, lead with it.

## Jargon: two kinds

- **Value-creating jargon** — community code-words. Using them signals
  membership and earns trust.
- **Value-destroying jargon** — show-off vocabulary with no community
  function.

The blanket "avoid jargon" advice is wrong. Use the community's; drop
your own.

## First-sentence test

Is there a slot for the reader in the first sentence — or only the writer
/ only the topic? Sharp diagnostic. Rewrite until there's a slot.

## "Why should I think that?"

Reader-perspective replacement for the teacher's "why do you think that?"
When defending any claim, ask not "why do I think X?" but "why should my
*reader* think X?"

## The reading-failure cascade

When the reader hits friction: slow down → reread → don't understand →
get annoyed → stop. Diagnostic: at which step is the reader bailing on
this draft?

## "Never tell the reader anything until they want to know it"

General principle. Don't introduce yourself until they want to know who
you are. Don't explain the product until they want to know what it is.
Don't define the term until they need the definition. Every information
unit is gated by reader-desire.

## Audience value codes (Nobel Prize pattern)

The Royal Swedish Academy publishes TWO statements for each Nobel — a
public one and an "Advanced Information" one. Both are for experts. The
difference isn't audience expertise; it's **value code**:

- **Pragmatic** community: "this matters because of what it lets us do."
- **Conceptual** community: "this matters because of what it lets us
  understand."

Same content, two completely different framings. Generalizable: when
selling the same product/idea to engineering vs operations vs executive
audiences, you likely need two drafts with two value codes, not one draft
with a different intro.

## Speech-only mechanics

For spoken work (podcasts, video scripts, sales calls, conference talks):
write for what the speaker can *say*. Some words and rhythms work on the
page but choke in delivery. Often you add words for rhythm, not cut them.
